PromptTTSD: Controllable Text to Spoken Dialogue Synthesis with Open-Ended Text Descriptions

Sulong Yang, Zhi Zeng, Xiupeng Wu, Juewen Hu, Pring Wong, Shuwu Zhang, Shuo Chen · Relevance 6.3

Spoken dialogue synthesis aims to generate multi-turn conversational speech from dialogue scripts while maintaining speaker consistency and contextual appropriateness. However, existing models largely lack fine-grained controllability, which is critical for capturing the dynamic, context-dependent style shifts in natural conversations. While prompt-based TTS has enabled such control for single-speaker speech, existing datasets heavily rely on predefined tags, which fundamentally limits their capacity to capture open-ended styles. To address this gap, we propose a scalable pipeline for extracting conversational speech from multi-narrator audiobooks, featuring an improved two-stage alignment algorithm to preserve dialogue continuity, paired with LLM-based extraction of style descriptions directly from narrative text. We release PromptDialog, a large-scale multi-speaker conversational speech dataset comprising 8,383 hours of Chinese speech and 120 hours of English speech, with open-ended style descriptions for 79% of utterances. We further introduce PromptTTSD, a spoken dialogue synthesis model built via a prompt-aware fine-tuning strategy that integrates style prompts into text-speech interleaved sequences. PromptTTSD is an open-source spoken dialogue synthesis model that supports both zero-shot voice cloning and natural language style control. Experiments demonstrate that PromptTTSD not only maintains strong fundamental synthesis capabilities but also achieves competitive open-ended style control compared with proprietary models, and substantially improves contextual appropriateness in multi-turn dialogues.

TLDR: The paper introduces PromptDialog, a large-scale multi-speaker conversational speech dataset with open-ended natural-language style descriptions mined from multi-narrator audiobooks, and PromptTTSD, an open-source prompt-aware spoken dialogue synthesis model that enables zero-shot voice cloning and natural-language style control while improving contextual appropriateness in multi-turn dialogues.

SLP-Bench: Evaluating and Improving Acoustic Claims of Reasoning Audio Language Models on Clinical Speech

C\^ome Hosxe, Rachid Riad, Quentin Spinat · Relevance 5.8

Audio language models (ALMs) can answer clinical questions from speech, outperforming baselines built on handcrafted acoustic features. Reasoning ALMs generate a chain of thought that refers to properties of the speaker's voice before predicting. Each statement the model makes about the voice needs to be checked against clinicians' perceptual ratings of the same recording. On clinical speech, existing evaluations score only the final answer. We introduce Speech Language Pathologist Bench (SLP-Bench), a benchmark grading each acoustic claim in the chain of thought against clinicians' perceptual ratings of the same recording. It covers five clinician-rated datasets and 25 perceptual dimensions, and assesses claims for factuality, stability under resampling, and consistency across reworded and different questions. Across ten open-weight reasoning ALMs evaluated zero-shot, claim balanced accuracy ranges from 0.52 to 0.58, claims change under resampling and rewording, and the most consistent models are not the most factual. Fine-tuning a 7B ALM with GRPO on a reward for claims that agree with clinicians raises its claim balanced accuracy from 0.54 to 0.60 and its mean kappa from 0.39 to 0.61, while preserving answer accuracy on per-dimension questions. SLP-Bench is available upon request, subject to approval by the providers of each dataset.

TLDR: SLP-Bench evaluates reasoning audio language models’ acoustic claims on clinical speech against clinician ratings, revealing limited and unstable zero-shot factuality that can be improved via GRPO fine-tuning with a clinician-agreement reward.

Efficient Conversational Multi-Talker ASR with Predicted Speaker-Agnostic Activity Streams

Xiluo He, Magdalena Rybicka, Laureano Moro-Velazquez, Jes\'us Villalba, Thomas Thebaud, Najim Dehak · Relevance 5.4

Multi-talker automatic speech recognition (ASR) can be performed by conditioning a single-speaker recognizer on frame-level speaker activity. However, this approach requires a complete diarization pass to obtain the conditioning signals and one ASR pass per speaker, making both the frontend and recognition costs dependent on the number of speakers. We reduce these costs by using Heuristic Error Assignment Training (HEAT) to merge the activities of an arbitrary number of speakers into two speaker-agnostic streams. Conditioning on these streams limits recognition to two ASR passes regardless of the number of participants. Through an analysis of the resulting encoder representations, we show that under a stream-assignment heuristic suited to conversational speech, the recognizer represents a stream target much as it does a speaker target. Because the streams do not encode persistent speaker identities, we further replace the conventional sliding-window diarization and clustering frontend with a model that predicts both activity streams directly from the audio in a single pass. A combination of arrival-order and permutation-invariant training objectives over the two-stream powerset labels prevents the predictor from collapsing to identical streams. We also investigate post-hoc speaker attribution by matching decoded utterances to speaker activities within their assigned streams. Experiments on corpora with diverse conversational dynamics demonstrate a favorable efficiency--accuracy tradeoff. On AMI and ICSI, two-stream conditioning is respectively 44\% and 87\% faster than per-speaker conditioning using the same diarization frontend, with less than 2\% relative WER degradation. Direct activity prediction increases these speedups to 79\% and 132\%, with relative WER degradations of 10\% and 21\%, respectively.

TLDR: The paper reduces conversational multi-talker ASR cost by merging any number of speakers into two speaker-agnostic activity streams via HEAT and directly predicting these streams in a single pass, limiting recognition to two ASR passes regardless of speaker count and achieving large speedups with modest WER degradation while also exploring post-hoc speaker attribution.

TLDR: Analyzing distinctive-feature distances in segmental triphones across 17 languages, the paper finds cross-linguistically recurring intermediate-distance, lower-entropy sequencing with stronger incoming–outgoing transition coupling than shuffled baselines, supporting contrastive dispersion as a constraint on phoneme transitions beyond simple consonant–vowel templates.

CoPyro: Feeding Copy Proposals to Parallel Drafters

Hong-Kai Zheng, Piji Li · Relevance 4.7

Speculative decoding accelerates language generation by letting a target model verify several draft tokens in one forward pass. Earlier neural drafters such as EAGLE extend each candidate sequence one token at a time. Recent work has turned to parallel drafters such as DFlash, which predict a whole block at once. Another approach uses n-gram retrieval to find an earlier occurrence of the current context suffix and copy the tokens that followed it directly into the draft. We introduce CoPyro, which feeds these copied tokens into a parallel drafter in place of masks. The drafter learns from context which tokens to keep and which to revise as it predicts the block in its usual forward pass. Across eight benchmarks on Qwen3-4B, CoPyro improves overall acceptance length for all four parallel drafters tested, with gains of 2.19-5.08% when trained on 100K samples. With DFlash trained on 710K samples, overall gains range from 1.08% to 2.00% across the tested model sizes and temperatures. Code generation benefits most in both settings. On Qwen3-8B, processing the proposals adds an average of 0.24 ms per decoding round in Transformers.

TLDR: TLDR: CoPyro improves parallel speculative decoding by feeding retrieved n-gram copy proposals into the drafter in place of masks, letting it learn which proposed tokens to keep or revise and thereby increasing acceptance length and throughput, especially for code generation.

Open CDLM: A Benchmark and Library for Continuous Diffusion Language Models

Hao Jiang, Ruixi Chen, Jiayi Han, Yulei Sun, Yujian Chen, Gen Li, Brian. K Chen, Guang Cheng, Weijian Luo, Tianyang Hu · Relevance 4.7

Reliable comparisons of continuous diffusion language models (CDLMs) require evaluating both model fit and the distribution of generated text. Two common practices leave this goal only partially addressed. Likelihood-based evaluation often reports a variational upper bound on discrete negative log-likelihood, whose slack can confound comparisons. Generated-sample-based evaluation commonly combines generative perplexity with within-sequence unigram entropy; both scores can remain favorable even when a sampler produces the same text on every draw. We introduce \textbf{Open CDLM}, a benchmark for evaluating CDLMs and their samplers, and describe the design of an accompanying open-source library to support reproducible comparisons. For likelihood-based evaluation, we clarify which comparisons of model fit are supported by reported perplexity bounds and how those conclusions depend on the evaluation procedure. For sample-based evaluation, we use \textit{Kernel Text Discrepancy} (\textbf{KTD}) to compare collections of generated and real texts, alongside diagnostics for diversity, validity, and coverage. In controlled OpenWebText experiments, replacing a collection of texts with copies of a single selected passage improves both conventional generation scores. MAUVE detects the resulting collapse, and its response to other perturbations depends on the text representation and the type of perturbation. This work marks the beginning of Open CDLM as an open-source project for CDLM research. We will maintain and extend the library over time and welcome contributions from the community.

TLDR: Open CDLM is a benchmark and open-source library for continuous diffusion language models that standardizes likelihood- and sample-based evaluation—clarifying perplexity-bound comparisons and introducing Kernel Text Discrepancy with diversity, validity, and coverage diagnostics—to enable reliable, reproducible comparisons of models and samplers.

Jonathan von Rad, Pontus Stenetorp, Yao Lu · Relevance 4.5

Tokenization represents one of the most crucial steps in mulilingual NLP pipelines. Yet, tokenizers routinely under-allocate vocabulary to low-resource languages. While improved allocation leads to better performance, it remains poorly understood how tokenization shapes cross-lingual representations and transfer during pretraining. To address this, we pretrain 39 mono- and bilingual 1B decoder-only models on FineWeb2-HQ across English, German, French, Arabic and Chinese, varying only the tokenizer. All of our checkpoints and code are publicly available.

TLDR: Fairer tokenizer vocabulary allocation in 39 pretrained 1B multilingual models increased cross-lingual representation alignment and feed-forward knowledge sharing but did not improve cross-lingual transfer, revealing an alignment-transfer dissociation and showing that cross-script transfer gaps persist beyond tokenization artifacts.

Analyzing frame similarity and the vision tokenizers of 16 open models, we trace this loss to a step before token selection. Under a token budget, the frames of a long video are sampled farther apart than the roughly 2 s over which frames remain similar. The two-frame 3D patches of models such as Qwen-VL then blend two unrelated scenes into each visual token, a double exposure that arises before any pruning or merging. Code is available at: https://github.com/zhkandsrz/ESSENCE.

TLDR: ESSENCE is a training-free video LLM accelerator that allocates the token budget before encoding by tokenizing temporally coherent “moments” rather than individual frames and adapting the reduction to the vision tokenizer class, achieving higher accuracy at matched or faster time-to-first-token.

Speculative Decoding Is Less Speculative Than You Think

Guanning Zeng, Andrea Zanette · Relevance 4.3

Speculative decoding accelerates language-model inference by preparing candidate continuations before the target model generates them. Its efficiency depends on how well limited drafting computation anticipates the path the target will actually follow. Anticipating this path involves two sources of uncertainty: the target model's predictions and the randomness of sampling. Yet the former requires a target-model forward pass, while the latter can be fixed before drafting. Building on this observation, we introduce DICE (Draw-Informed Candidate Selection), which pre-samples and synchronizes verification randomness across drafting and verification. By combining these known draws with predictions from an existing drafter, DICE allocates computation toward continuations more likely to be used in the current sampling run.
