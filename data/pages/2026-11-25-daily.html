Scoring L2 Pronunciation Without Synthesis: Counterfactual Velocity Contrast in Frozen Flow-Matching TTS

Zehan Liu · Relevance 6.1

Flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS) models learn how native speakers realize a text, yet this knowledge is used only for synthesis. We show that a frozen TTS model can score non-native (L2) pronunciation without generating audio. For a learner utterance, we compare the model's velocity-prediction error under the target text with its error under a counterfactual text (dropped or swapped) on the same noised input; the difference behaves like a generative goodness-of-pronunciation score. The signal is confined to a narrow band of high-noise timesteps, which a native-speech criterion locates and a closed-form rule transfers across backbones. On speaker-disjoint speechocean762, four frozen TTS models reach $r{=}0.58$--$0.63$ with human scores, within the range of zero-shot ASR and phone-level baselines, and adding them to those baselines in a label-free fusion raises $r$ from $0.698$ to $0.757$. Cached frame scores also flag word-level errors at no extra TTS cost.

TLDR: The paper shows that a frozen flow-matching TTS model can score L2 pronunciation without generating audio by contrasting its velocity-prediction error under the target text with that under a counterfactual text on the same noised speech, using a transferable high-noise timestep band to achieve human-score correlations comparable to zero-shot baselines and improve them when fused.

Tenlai-TTS 2.0: Continuous-Representation LLM-Based TTS System with End-to-End Training

Changfeng Gao, Yong Ren, Jun Yuan, Zhengkun Tian, Ye Bai, Zhao You, Shidong Shang · Relevance 6.1

Autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) with continuous speech latents avoids quantization-induced information loss, but typically relies on a latent space that is learned separately and then frozen before language-model training. Such a space must satisfy two competing requirements: it must retain enough acoustic detail for high-fidelity reconstruction, yet be sufficiently structured and regularized to support autoregressive prediction by a language model. We present Tenlai-TTS 2.0, which learns continuous speech representations through end-to-end training to balance acoustic detail with autoregressive predictability. The model comprises a speech encoder with a capacity-controlled Gaussian bottleneck, a large language model (LLM) with an autoregressive diffusion transformer (AR-DiT) head, a flow-matching (FM) mel-spectrogram decoder, and a multi-task recognition model (RM). With energy normalization and fixed-variance Gaussian noise, the bottleneck has a closed-form upper bound on its information rate. Through joint optimization with reconstruction, recognition, and next-patch prediction objectives, we learn continuous speech representations that suit all three tasks and train the LLM and AR-DiT for autoregressive generation. The LLM and AR-DiT are further optimized using differentiable reconstruction and recognition feedback on their predicted latents through the FM decoder and RM. On Seed-TTS-eval, this feedback-based optimization reduces character error rate (CER) from 0.82% to 0.74% on test-zh and from 8.32% to 7.08% on test-hard-zh, and word error rate (WER) from 1.32% to 1.27% on test-en, compared with joint training alone. Subsequent reinforcement learning further reduces CER to 6.29% on test-hard-zh and WER to 1.08% on test-en, with speaker similarity scores of 0.811, 0.766, and 0.784 on test-zh, test-en, and test-hard-zh, respectively.

TLDR: Tenlai-TTS 2.0 is a continuous-representation LLM-based TTS system that learns its speech latent end-to-end under a capacity-controlled Gaussian bottleneck, jointly training the encoder, LLM/AR-DiT, flow-matching decoder, and recognition model with differentiable reconstruction and recognition feedback to balance acoustic fidelity with autoregressive predictability.

BASE: A Backend-Agnostic Streaming Embodiment Layer for Full-Duplex Conversational Avatars

Chenye Yang, Jin Jiang, Shengcai Liao · Relevance 6.1

Many end-to-end spoken dialogue models can now converse in real time and full duplex, and turning them into 3D avatars requires full-body gestures, listening feedback, and turn-taking. Existing methods couple the motion generator tightly to one dialogue model by reading its internal states or training speech and motion jointly. The motion module must then be retrained whenever the dialogue model changes, cannot be attached to cloud APIs that expose only audio, and entangles role, voice, and motion style. We propose BASE, a backend-agnostic streaming embodiment layer whose only interface to the dialogue model is its emitted speech. First, a conversation state manager driven solely by audio handles speaking, listening, idling, barge-in, and proactive turns on one timeline, while role, voice, motion style, and 3D appearance are set independently. Second, to run an offline 2 s flow-matching gesture model on live audio, we reformulate inference as sliding-window continuation with overlapped look-ahead ensembling, which removes seam jitter. Third, we show that the remaining streaming gap comes from errors accumulating in the self-generated motion history rather than missing future speech; our Drift-Aware History Perturbation (DAHP) reduces this gap in about 50 min on one GPU by injecting inference-like drift into ground-truth training histories, and transfers to a second generator. On BEAT2, streaming BASE reaches an FGD of 0.342, on par with the tightly coupled MIBURI (0.333), and viewers prefer its body motion over MIBURI's in 82% of forced-choice judgments. Without retraining, the same layer drives a cascaded pipeline, three open full-duplex models, and a cloud API in real time.

TLDR: BASE is a backend-agnostic streaming embodiment layer that turns any dialogue model’s emitted speech into real-time full-duplex 3D avatar motion—including gestures, listening, and turn-taking—without retraining when the backend changes.

BiSAT: Bidirectionally Aligned Semantic-Acoustic Tokenization for Speech Language Models

Y. Lu, L. Wang, S. Okada · Relevance 6.1

Speech language models (LMs) generate speech as sequences of discrete tokens. Their tokenizer must therefore preserve both acoustic detail and linguistic content at a low bitrate. Semantic-enhanced tokenizers may lose acoustic detail, whereas acoustic-oriented tokenizers retain acoustic variation that makes language modeling harder. Unified tokenizers combine both, but representing acoustic detail and linguistic content within a single feature space can cause interference between the two objectives, and existing approaches typically align acoustic representations to fixed semantic targets from a frozen teacher. We propose Bidirectionally Aligned Semantic-Acoustic Tokenization (BiSAT), a unified tokenization framework with separate acoustic and semantic branches aligned by a shared-index mechanism. This design learns acoustic and semantic representations in separate continuous spaces while producing a single discrete code sequence, and it jointly optimizes both encoders without relying on fixed semantic teacher targets. Based on BiSAT, we develop BiSAT-TTS, a zero-shot text-to-speech system comprising an autoregressive text-speech LM and a conditional flow-matching acoustic decoder. Reconstruction, downstream understanding, and diagnostic analyses show that BiSAT effectively preserves semantic and acoustic information in the code stream, performs well on in-the-wild and out-of-domain speech, and learns complementary codebook representations. The frozen-code understanding results also support its feasibility and potential for code-based reward modeling in reinforcement learning post-training. BiSAT-TTS performs comparably to state-of-the-art speech LM-based TTS baselines with less training data, further supporting BiSAT's effectiveness for speech language modeling. The audio demos are available at https://seekmind-lab.github.io/bisat-demo.

TLDR: BiSAT is a unified speech tokenizer that learns separate acoustic and semantic representations coupled through a shared-index mechanism into a single low-bitrate code stream, jointly optimizing both branches without fixed semantic teachers and enabling competitive zero-shot TTS via BiSAT-TTS.

SpeechSplice: Pronunciation Control in Encoder-Decoder LLM-based TTS by Replacing Encoder Hidden States

Kiyoshi Kurihara, Jumpei Ukita, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yutaka Matsuo · Relevance 6.0

Most large language model (LLM)-based text-to-speech (TTS) methods offer no explicit function for correcting pronunciation. Consequently, these systems frequently mispronounce many everyday words, such as proper nouns, heteronyms, numerals, and domain-specific terms. While weight-editing or fine-tuning can mitigate this, these solutions risk quality degradation and must operate offline. We propose SpeechSplice, a training-free encoder-output replacement approach for pronunciation control in encoder-decoder LLM-based TTS. SpeechSplice replaces the encoder-output hidden states of a word with those of its intended reading. Cross-architecture and cross-lingual experiments cover ten public TTS systems in 17 model-language conditions. At the hidden state we replace, the intervention moves pronunciation toward the target reading more often when the generator reads the text through cross-attention than when text and speech share one self-attention sequence. In human evaluations, native-language listeners identified speech generated with SpeechSplice as realizing the target reading in 98.6-100.0% of judgments, while a separate study found no significant naturalness difference from unmodified synthesis. By clarifying the conditions under which pronunciation correction succeeds, this study offers an empirical basis for investigating pronunciation control in LLM-based TTS architectures.

TLDR: SpeechSplice is a training-free pronunciation-control method for encoder–decoder LLM-based TTS that replaces a target word’s encoder hidden states with those of its intended reading, achieving near-perfect native-listener target-reading recognition without significant naturalness loss, especially when the decoder reads text via cross-attention.

Vladislav Nesterenko, Polina Doronicheva, Olga Golubeva, Polina Sheviakova · Relevance 5.7

Speech large language models are usually evaluated on questions that have answers. We ask what they do when a recording establishes none, and whether the standard way of finding out is sound. Diagnosed on synthesized speech the answer looks clear: Qwen2-Audio fabricates an answer to 92.5% of unanswerable questions, while an ASR cascade reading the same recordings reaches 2.5% hallucination at 6.7% over-refusal, placing the failure in reasoning rather than in perception. Since no prompt we tested repairs the residual failure, we transfer pre-generation attention probing to the multimodal setting. A probe over frozen prompt representations reaches 0.773 AUROC at predicting which unanswerable questions the model will answer anyway, where semantic entropy over ten sampled generations is at chance: the model fabricates the same answer every time, so sample disagreement cannot see a failure it is confident about.

TLDR: This paper shows that synthesized speech can reverse conclusions about whether speech LLMs hallucinate on unanswerable audio, and proposes a pre-generation probing method that predicts when the model will answer anyway with 0.773 AUROC, outperforming sampling-based uncertainty.

Synthetic Twins: Closing the Gap Between TTS Test Sets and Real Recordings in Turkish ASR Evaluation

Nur Ya\u{g}mur Tuncer, Mustafa Serta\c{c} T\"urkel, Fatma Nur Korkmaz, Ahmet Tu\u{g}rul Bayrak · Relevance 5.6

Speech synthesized by text-to-speech engines is a low-cost substitute for recorded test sets in automatic speech recognition, yet an off-the-shelf synthetic test set is worth only a few dozen real recordings for estimating the real error rate of a recognizer. This paper examines which properties of real recordings are missing from synthetic speech and how much of the gap can be closed. For 507 Turkish Common Voice recordings, synthetic twins are built: a ladder of synthetic copies that adds, one factor at a time, the pitch of the speaker, a clone of the same speaker, the real recording channel and the speaking rate. At each step, the agreement between ASR behaviour on the copy and on the real recording is measured. Across three zero-shot cloning engines and panels of six and 19 recognizers, the speaker clone is the step that improves agreement on which sentences are hard, raising the Spearman correlation from .41 to .55 for Chatterbox. Matching the channel or adding generic noise corrects the error level when the synthetic speech is easier than real speech. The twin with the highest worth is equivalent to 70 to 122 real recordings, depending on the recognizer panel, compared with about 9 for a stock voice. The findings replicate on VoxForge, a second corpus in which speaker identity is known rather than inferred: the clone raises sentence agreement from .31 to .50 and the worth from 8 to about 50 recordings. Agreement on which sentences are hard is distinct from agreement on how hard they are. The paper concludes with a practical recipe and a reporting checklist for synthetic ASR test sets.

TLDR: A stepwise “synthetic twin” approach shows that speaker cloning most improves agreement between TTS-based and real Turkish ASR evaluation at the sentence-difficulty level, while channel matching corrects error-level bias and substantially increases how many real recordings a synthetic test set is worth.
