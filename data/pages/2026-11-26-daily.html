Reproducing the Speech Labels of Qwen3-Omni's Talker: Mimi in bf16, and a Last-Frame Pitfall

Yuxuan Chen · Relevance 5.9

Qwen3-Omni speaks through a Talker predicting 16 codebooks of a 12.5 Hz audio codec, but the encoder that made its training labels was not released, so new voices cannot be trained on matching labels. We identify this encoder from the released model's behavior. White-box regression shows that the codebook embeddings of the Talker, its multi-token predictor and the Code2Wav decoder match the first 16 codebooks of kyutai's Mimi layer by layer. A decoder-based test looks convincing but fails calibration: decoders trained on known labels cannot tell them apart. Teacher-forced Talker likelihoods, calibrated with 13 code language models trained on known labels, do tell implementations apart: the Talker prefers labels from kyutai's moshi package with bf16 weights over every other variant we tried. Loudness normalization and band-limiting of 20% or more of the data are ruled out. Separately, transformers' MimiModel encodes the last frame differently from kyutai's encoder unless the input length is a multiple of 1920 samples. After LoRA fine-tuning on its labels, 44--55% of generated sentences end in a noise burst, and the same frame costs the Mimi-based CSM-1B 119 nats; padding the input removes both. We release the encoding recipe, a label checker and a Talker fine-tuning toolkit.

TLDR: The paper identifies Qwen3-Omni Talker’s unreleased speech-label encoder as kyutai’s Mimi (first 16 codebooks, run via `moshi` in bf16), exposes a transformers `MimiModel` last-frame bug that causes noise bursts after fine-tuning unless inputs are padded to a multiple of 1920 samples, and releases an encoding recipe, label checker, and Talker fine-tuning toolkit.

Real-world speech is often partially observable: audio may be missing or corrupted by noise and competing speakers, while the speaker's lips remain visible. Generating Schema-Aligned Synthetic Dialogues and Measuring Their Diversity

Konrad Stolte, Toni Fetzer · Relevance 5.5

Training or evaluating a model that turns a conversation into a structured record requires paired data, meaning a transcript together with the exact record it should be converted into. Such pairs are expensive to annotate, and in sensitive domains they are also difficult to obtain at all. We therefore build them in the opposite order by sampling a schema-valid record first, then writing a dialogue from it, and optionally synthesizing speech from the dialogue, so the label exists before the text and we need no annotation step. Naively prompting a language model for such dialogues yields small variations of a single conversation, so we add a sampled variation configuration, personas, and a topic plan that orders the fields of the record as explicit mechanisms for variation. We release the generation library and the generated corpus of German clinical dialogues with aligned records.

TLDR: The paper generates schema-aligned synthetic dialogues by first sampling a valid structured record and then writing a dialogue from it, adding variation mechanisms (sampled configurations, personas, and topic plans) to substantially increase embedding-space diversity and improve downstream field extraction over naive generation.

Phoneme-identity depth profiles do not reliably predict LoRA placement for dysarthric ASR

Bernard Muller, LaVonne Roberts · Relevance 5.0

Parameter-efficient adaptation of a speech foundation model to dysarthric speech requires a rarely justified choice: which encoder layers to adapt. A recently proposed strategy is to probe the encoder, find where phonetic information becomes most decodable, and put the adapters there. We test that inference across two ASR models (Qwen3-ASR, Whisper-small) and two dysarthric corpora (Mandarin CDSD, English UASpeech), holding recipe and seed discipline constant. The inference fails. Measured under one pipeline rather than inherited from published figures, all four phoneme-error curves minimise between 83% and 92% of relative depth, each model's minimising layer moving no further between corpora than between probe seeds. The best adaptation band is not similarly constant: none of three natural mappings from the curve to a band -- steepest improvement, lowest band-mean error, or knee location -- recovers the best tested configuration. We do not claim no statistic of the curve could work, only that these three do not. Placement still matters, but is model- and corpus-specific. On CDSD the models disagree -- Qwen3-ASR prefers its upper third (29.88% CER, three-seed mean), Whisper-small all-block LoRA (53.56%) -- while on UASpeech neither favours the upper band. At three seeds, upper-versus-all is resolved (-1.31 pp, CI [-2.16, -0.25]) but upper-versus-middle is not (-0.83 pp, CI [-2.06, +1.86]). The two models' preferences differ as a directly tested interaction (-4.71 pp, CI [-6.17, -2.89]); we designate this the primary claim, and record that the designation was made post hoc. On Qwen3-ASR x UASpeech, adapting near the input is reliably worse than not adapting at all (+9.90 pp CER). We recommend choosing the band by a short adaptation sweep on the model and corpus in hand, not by reading it off a probe.

TLDR: This paper shows that phoneme-identity depth profiles do not reliably predict LoRA adapter placement for dysarthric ASR across Qwen3-ASR and Whisper-small on CDSD and UASpeech; because optimal placement is model- and corpus-specific, the authors recommend selecting the adaptation band via a short empirical sweep rather than from a probe.

Evaluations of quantized language models attribute the change in a benchmark score to quantization. We write the measured change as the true change plus a term contributed by the instrument. Instrument error that is the same at every precision can shrink a paired comparison but cannot create or reverse an effect. Error that interacts with quantization can do both, and it is reported as an effect of quantization. We estimate this interaction for four measurement choices in 8 instruction-tuned models below two billion parameters, at float16, LLM.int8() and NF4, on identical items. Measured against labels from two human annotators, the 4-bit rise in harmful compliance is about half to two-thirds of the rise in non-refusal that a corrected key word detector records. Cloze and lettered multiple-choice scoring disagree about the effect of quantization far more than their noise allows: their estimates correlate at \r{ho} = 0.10, against a ceiling of about 0.77. LLM.int8() answers and generations depend on which items share a batch, and padding alone changes its answers. Rounding activations to 8 bits is enough to produce this dependence. NF4 is batch-invariant. The two largest reported calibration losses are response-bias shifts. We release item-level predictions, option scores and human annotations, and give recommendations for evaluating compressed small models.

TLDR: This paper shows that trustworthiness evaluations of quantized small language models often misattribute measurement artifacts—such as scoring-method disagreements, batch/padding dependence, and response-bias shifts—to quantization, and it releases item-level data plus recommendations for more robust evaluation.

Chain-of-thought (CoT) allows language models to verbalise multi-step rationales before producing their final answer. While this technique often boosts task performance and offers an impression of transparency into the model's reasoning, this position paper argues that rationales generated by current CoT techniques can be misleading and are neither necessary nor sufficient for trustworthy interpretability. We analyse faithfulness as whether CoTs are not only human-interpretable but also reflect the model's underlying reasoning well enough to support responsible use. We argue that this principle reflects CLIP's visual features rather than a property of the task. We provide a simple Bayesian interpretation, in which a class name is worth exactly kappa = sigma^2 / tau^2 labeled images, the ratio between the visual spread of a class and the misalignment of its name. A masked diffusion language model is fast because it commits several tokens per step. Each token is sampled from its own marginal, so the dependency between the tokens is lost, and the size of that loss is the conditional total correlation C of the committed set. Most decoders choose the set from per-position marginals, which can bound C but cannot determine it. We ask how well they predict it. On three of the four, the best marginal-only predictor is off by about one bit (0.74 to 1.17), 1.8 to 3.7 times less than a baseline that knows only the bound, and most of this accuracy comes from the bound itself. Among six common marginal rules, pooling over the set beats gating on its weakest position in all 24 paired comparisons. The measurement needs a validated readout: Dream and LLaDA place a position's prediction at different logit indices, and the wrong index inflates an inconsistency diagnostic 14 to 41 times. We give three checks that measure the convention. Our results concern predicting C for a fixed set, not what a dependency-aware decoder gains.

TLDR: **TLDR:** This paper shows that marginal-only rules in masked diffusion LM parallel decoding can predict the conditional total correlation lost by multi-token commits only to within about one bit—largely via a simple entropy bound—and that how marginals are pooled matters more than which marginal statistic is used, while correct logit-index readout must be validated.

Worth the Wait? How Response Time and Streaming Shape Judgments of Generative AI Answers and Chatbot Engagement

Zihan Chen, Di Zhu, Lei Nico Zheng · Relevance 4.0

Answers from generative AI chatbots built on large language models (LLMs) now arrive at very different speeds: reasoning models deliberate before replying, while response caches can return a stored answer at once. Blind preference votes, which rank these models, are read as judgments of their text, and if delivery matters, faster answers are expected to gain. Research on the labor illusion and on how text appears on screen suggests instead that an answer which unfolds as it is written is credited over the same answer shown at once. We test these accounts with three sources of variation in natural use. First, compar:IA, a French government chatbot arena, answered repeated prompts, by a random draw, either with a fresh answer streamed as it was written or with a stored earlier answer from the same model, shown at once. Within the same prompt, a replay lowered an answer's chance of winning a decisive blind vote by 25.8 percentage points. The same stored text won 57% of its decisive votes when streamed and 29% when replayed, and voters called it less complete, more superficial and more often incorrect when replayed. Second, provider congestion slowed streamed answers. In 324,029 conversations, slower answers earned no detectable net credit in the vote, but a slower first exchange made visitors less likely to ask a follow-up question. Third, in 4,378,514 WildChat replies from everyday ChatGPT use, replies slowed by congestion made users less likely to continue. Delivery moved blind votes, in the direction opposite to the one the arena's designers expected, while waiting cost follow-up questions that a comparison between two answers cannot register when both are slow. The results bear on how AI services cache and stream answers, how they budget latency, and how preference leaderboards evaluate models.

TLDR: **TLDR:** Showing the same generative-AI answer as a streamed response rather than an instant replay sharply increases blind preference votes, while slower responses earn no net vote credit but reduce users’ likelihood of continuing the conversation—showing that delivery timing and streaming shape AI judgments and engagement in ways leaderboards miss.
