An LLM Is All You Need: Data-Efficient Adaptation to Full-Duplex, Word-Level Spoken Dialogue

Tairan Chen, Richard Yucheng He, Yihang Liu, Baodong Cao, Chen Xu · Relevance 6.5

Full-duplex spoken dialogue, in which a system listens while it speaks and can backchannel, yield the floor, and be interrupted, is usually obtained by training on hundreds of thousands to millions of hours of speech. This is costly, rewrites the language model that supplies the system's knowledge, and still leaves turn-taking either implicit or decided coarsely rather than word by word. We show that a frozen, general-purpose multimodal LLM can instead be adapted into a word-level full-duplex system with little data. Our system is a thinker-talker cascade on an 80 ms frame clock. The thinker keeps the LLM frozen and trains only lightweight adapters and a few new token embeddings (97M parameters); at every frame it emits one token of agent text, with pauses, yields, and onsets represented as tokens of the same vocabulary and kept legal by a finite-state machine. A four-stage curriculum, each stage anchored to the model's own behavior under the previous format, performs the conversion in about 30 GPU-hours. The talker reads only the thinker's discrete tokens, so the two halves are trained independently. On Full-Duplex-Bench and InteractionBench, the system is competitive with open-source full-duplex systems built through large-scale speech training: among them, it is the least likely to take the floor when the user merely pauses, backchannels most often, and leads in spoken-task accuracy, groundedness, and coherence over long conversations.

TLDR: The paper shows that a frozen general-purpose multimodal LLM can be adapted with lightweight modules and a ~30-GPU-hour four-stage curriculum into a word-level full-duplex spoken dialogue system that competes with systems trained on large-scale speech data.

RSE-TTS: Compact Text-to-Speech through Shared Text-Aware Reference Encoding and Generation

Ming Ma, Mingjie Chen, Yuanzhong Zheng, Hengyao Bao, Hailin Yu, Shijing Yu, Hao Qiu, Gongfan Cao, Xiao Li, Xuemin Zhao, Youhai Jiang · Relevance 6.1

Can a speech generator also serve as an effective voice encoder? We show that a pretrained autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) backbone can be repurposed for reference compression, improving speaker similarity in subsequent synthesis. Jointly encoding reference speech and its transcript further strengthens this capability. Controlled ablations and activation interventions trace the text benefit through intermediate acoustic representations into the compressed vectors that condition generation. Building on these findings, we introduce RSE-TTS, whose Reconstructive Speaker Encoder (RSE) shares the generation backbone and compresses paired reference text and speech into eight continuous vectors. Sharing reduces encoder-generator parameters by 48% relative to independent pretrained encoding. Large-scale English training yields a 118M-parameter generator that improves both word error rate and speaker similarity over the corresponding official MOSS-TTS-Nano cloning modes on all three English benchmarks. Combined with acoustic continuation, RSE-TTS approaches the speaker similarity of substantially larger systems while achieving lower WER than the evaluated CosyVoice 3 and MOSS-TTS-Local models. Audio samples are available at https://voxnexusai.github.io/RSE-TTS/.

TLDR: RSE-TTS is a compact TTS system that reuses a pretrained autoregressive TTS backbone as a shared text-aware reference encoder, compressing paired reference speech and transcript into eight continuous vectors to improve speaker similarity and intelligibility while reducing encoder–generator parameters by 48%.

Kezek: Splitting Mono Two-Person Conversations into Per-Speaker Tracks for Full-Duplex Dialogue Data

Bagzhan Karl, Alizhan Karl · Relevance 6.0

Full-duplex spoken dialogue models learn turn-taking, overlaps and backchannels from conversations recorded with one channel per speaker, but most available conversational audio is a single mono mix. We present Kezek, a model that turns a mono recording of exactly two people into one track per person with the original length and timing; overlaps and short replies ("yeah", "mm-hm") stay on the track of the person who said them. Kezek decides who speaks when and who is who once for the whole recording (stage 1: a two-voice activity model guided by a speaker fingerprint trained to be insensitive to phase), and a conditioned time-domain separator only carries out that decision (stage 2), with outputs that always sum to the input. Standard separation metrics miss the failures that matter for dialogue data, so we report short-reply retention, leakage onto the other person's solo, channel swaps and coverage. Against the open pipeline pyannote -> DialogueSidon on the same recordings, Kezek keeps more short replies (0.83 vs. 0.69 on Open Yap test; 0.80 vs. 0.71 over the other's speech on unseen telephone speech from CallHome English), mostly because it returns the whole recording while the external pipeline drops part of it (on the audio both return, reply retention is about the same: 0.859 vs. 0.865 on Open Yap); it also leaks less, swaps channels less often, and runs 4-5 times faster (about 230 hours of audio per GPU-hour). We also report negative results and an ideal-mask ceiling that locate the remaining gap: quiet replies under a louder voice. Code and weights are released under permissive licences.

TLDR: Kezek is a two-stage model that splits a mono two-person conversation into time-aligned per-speaker tracks by first deciding speaker activity and identity for the whole recording and then using a conditioned separator constrained to sum to the input, preserving overlaps and short replies while outperforming a pyannote→DialogueSidon pipeline and running 4–5× faster.

DeALR: Decoupled Audio-Latent Optimization with Acoustic Backtracking for Test-Time Reasoning

Haoyu He, Yan Rong, Li Liu · Relevance 5.6

Large audio-language models (LALMs) typically encode audio once and conduct subsequent multi-step reasoning in a language-dominated space, weakening access to fine-grained acoustic evidence. Although recent multimodal approaches have explored re-engaging perceptual inputs during reasoning, whether a frozen LALM can actively revisit crucial acoustic evidence in the latent space at test time remains underexplored. To bridge this gap, we introduce Decoupled Audio Latent Reasoning (DeALR), a novel test-time latent reasoning framework that shifts the paradigm from text-dominated deliberation to active audio-latent interaction. Inspired by the human cognitive process of "backtracking" to specific acoustic cues during complex listening tasks, DeALR establishes a dynamic interaction between learnable thought tokens and acoustic segments. Specifically, we introduce Span-Level Acoustic Backtracking, which traces initial attention distributions to dynamically isolate contiguous, key acoustic spans. Furthermore, we propose Decoupled Audio-Latent Optimization (DALO), a test-time reinforcement learning algorithm that jointly refines thought vectors and acoustic patches via confidence-guided policy gradients. Crucially, DALO explicitly decouples trajectory search from response generation by reinstating uncorrupted, high-fidelity audio embeddings prior to text synthesis, entirely eliminating optimization-induced distribution drift while retaining the navigation of the refined thoughts. Comprehensive experiments on benchmarks demonstrate that DeALR consistently outperforms prior test-time optimization approaches, delivering substantial improvements in both confidence calibration and reasoning accuracy.

TLDR: DeALR is a training-free test-time latent reasoning framework for frozen large audio-language models that uses span-level acoustic backtracking and decoupled confidence-guided optimization of thought tokens and audio patches—while restoring high-fidelity audio embeddings before decoding—to improve acoustic grounding, confidence calibration, and reasoning accuracy.

Low Language Transfer Speech-to-Text

Husein Zolkepli · Relevance 5.6

A multilingual speech recogniser has heard much speech in its own languages. We ask how much of that it brings to a language it was never trained on, when it is fine-tuned on the little that language has. We score 28 open multilingual models untouched on Low-Language-TTS, 50 low-resource languages in 11 writing systems, and fine-tune 24 of them on at most 1,000 clips a language, 82 hours in all, with a dev set choosing every epoch. Fine-tuning lowers every model's macro character error rate (CER), by 16.6 to 53.1 points, to 9.5 at best, and a word language model takes three CTC models 1.2 to 1.4 points lower; WER is still 34.4 at best. On the 12 languages that no model lists, every model starts at 46 or worse and the best ends at 11.7. The same architectures from random weights stay 60 points or more behind, so the gain is transfer, and an encoder left untouched already gives a Whisper or Qwen decoder most of what it needs. It is the language, not the corpus: on FLEURS recordings that ours do not share, the median model keeps 79% of its gain. It costs the languages the models knew, 5.7 points of CER on English, Spanish, French and German in the median model. Low-rank adapters on 4% of the weights come within 1.1 points of tuning everything, and on 2 of 3 models forget less. Seq2seq and language-model decoders are done within three to five epochs, while a new CTC head is still improving at twenty or thirty. Data beats epochs: 25 clips a language take Qwen3-ASR 0.6B from 60.9 to 39.3, and at equal clips presented, ten times the data is worth 9.7 points. A model that first learned forty of the languages learns the other ten no faster. On a public Malay benchmark, untouched Whisper large-v3 scores 8.9 when it detects the language and 14.9 when told the clip is Malay, because it then translates the English that speakers mix in; trained on the Scicom telephony dataset it scores 6.0.

TLDR: Fine-tuning open multilingual speech recognizers on as few as 1,000 clips per low-resource language produces large, transfer-driven character-error reductions—even for languages never seen during pretraining—while low-rank adapters approach full tuning and data quantity matters more than epochs, though adaptation degrades performance on the models’ original languages.

Scaffolding Self-Transduction with Alignment Attention Mask for Aligner-Encoder ASR

Jaeyoung Lee, Masato Mimura · Relevance 5.5

End-to-end ASR systems must jointly solve speech- text alignment and token prediction from sequence-level super- vision. We study this problem in Aligner-Encoder ASR, where alignment is performed inside encoder self-attention and can thus be directly intervened on with an attention mask. We use forced alignment to build an attention mask that confines selected encoder self-attention heads to a narrow window around the alignment path. The mask is faded out over training, leaving a deployable model with an unmodified inference architecture; alternatively, kept active at training and inference, it serves as an oracle that measures recognition performance once alignment is externally resolved. On LibriSpeech, the faded alignment mask improves test-clean/test-other WER from 3.9/6.4 to 3.5/5.9 over an Aligner baseline. This indicates that alignment inference is a major bottleneck in Aligner-Encoder ASR, and suggests that improved alignment mechanisms might yield substantial accuracy gains for end-to-end ASR.

TLDR: TLDR: By using forced alignment to build an attention mask that constrains selected encoder self-attention heads during Aligner-Encoder ASR training—then fading it out for deployment—the authors improve LibriSpeech WER, while oracle masks reveal that alignment inference is a major remaining bottleneck.

MuViRL: Multimodal View-Invariant Visual Speech Representation Learning via Calibrated View Aggregation

Fei Su, Kai Qiu, Juan Liu · Relevance 5.5

Multimodal representation learning combines complementary signals to capture shared semantic information. For dynamic scenes, these representations need to remain consistent across views while preserving distinctions between temporal states. In visual speech representation learning, view variation changes the visibility and reliability of articulation cues, making it difficult to learn such representations from synchronized audio and video. To address this challenge, we propose MuViRL, a self-supervised framework for multimodal view-invariant visual speech representation learning via calibrated view aggregation. The framework combines synchronized 3D Gaussian view synthesis with multimodal self-supervision to learn consistent representations from real and synthesized data. A multimodal decision model learns relative view utility from teacher prediction losses, and its calibrated scores guide the aggregation of complementary information. Experiments on real and synthesized multiview data demonstrate that MuViRL improves multimodal representation consistency and visual speech recognition performance across views.

TLDR: MuViRL is a self-supervised framework that learns view-invariant visual speech representations by combining synchronized 3D Gaussian view synthesis with calibrated aggregation of multimodal views, teacher-loss-based view-utility scoring, aggregation-to-single distillation, and temporal real–synthetic alignment to improve cross-view visual speech recognition.

Beyond Word Error Rate: A Failure Taxonomy of Streaming Dictation ASR

Harras Mansoor, Muhammad Wisal, Hassan Bhatti, Masood Shakir · Relevance 5.5

Speech recognition (ASR) systems are usually compared by word error rate (WER) on recorded files, after punctuation, capital letters and number format are removed. In dictation, the audio is streamed live and the text goes into the user's message as written, so what matters is which mistakes the user has to fix. The test set, streamed to eleven ASR systems, comprises 106 scripted English dictations from one speaker (read in a quiet room, with a fan on, and whispered), 20 clips in each of eleven other language and dialect sets, and 400 recordings that join clips in two languages. Every error was sorted by a fixed rule into four levels: recognition, output formatting, language handling and, for the self-hosted models, audio dropped before the model. Systems with similar WER failed in different ways. Zoom Scribe and OpenAI's GPT-Live-Transcribe have almost the same WER on quiet English (7.4% and 7.3%), but 34.7% and 8.8% on whispered English. Zoom and both AssemblyAI models left out 26-28% of whispered words, and still 21-26% with their end-of-turn detection relaxed or off; seven other systems left out at most 3%. Qwen3-ASR's 32% came from the speech detection in our own deployment (6% when relaxed). Across 107 EARS speakers, the same four systems lost 7-31% of whispered words, while six others lost under 2%. With only English selected, Gemini, Deepgram and our self-hosted Whisper lost the non-English part of a two-language recording in 86-99% of recordings, against 33-46% with both languages selected, while GPT-Live-Transcribe, OpenAI's realtime Whisper and Meta lost it in at most 20%. Running Whisper behind a Silero voice activity detector that drops audio judged to be silence raised whispered WER from 10.1% to 51.4%. Most English results come from one read speaker. The code, the recordings, both references and every transcript are released.

TLDR: This paper introduces a failure taxonomy for streaming dictation ASR that goes beyond word error rate and shows, across eleven systems, that systems with similar WER can fail in very different ways—especially on whispered speech, multilingual input, formatting, and pre-model audio dropping.

Speech Tokenizers are Vulnerable: Transferable Semantic Attack and Robust Tokenizer

Speech tokenizers serve as speech front ends, encoding input speech signals into representations for downstream models, such as automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems and large audio-language models (LALMs). In this paper, we find that speech tokenizers are fragile at the semantic level: small perturbations can disrupt their semantic encoding process and consequently cause severe degradation in downstream ASR and LALM performance. To study this vulnerability, we propose T-SemAttack, a transferable semantic attack based on a semantic encoder ensemble. By jointly drifting the representation spaces of multiple semantic encoders, T-SemAttack effectively disrupts the semantic content of speech while preserving perceptual quality, thereby inducing error features. We further analyze layer-wise representation drift and cross-model gradient alignment. Our findings show that perturbations can induce substantial shifts in deep semantic representations and that the input gradients of different surrogate encoders exhibit directional alignment. These observations suggest that shared adversarially sensitive directions in the input space may be a key factor underlying cross-model attack transferability. Extensive experiments on multiple datasets, 9 open-source and black-box ASR systems, and 4 LALMs demonstrate that T-SemAttack achieves strong transferable attack performance, while the proposed Robust Speech Tokenizer exhibits robustness under high-fidelity reconstruction and downstream tasks. Our code and demo are available at https://wxzyd123.github.io/t-semattack.

TLDR: The paper demonstrates that speech tokenizers are semantically vulnerable to small transferable adversarial perturbations—introduced via **T-SemAttack**, an ensemble-based semantic representation drifting attack—and proposes **ROSETok**, a robust speech tokenizer using noisy training and robust semantic distillation to preserve reconstruction fidelity and downstream ASR/LALM robustness.

Convolutional VocalTrax: Replacing Backpropagation Through Time with Convolution in Differentiable Articulatory Synthesis

Yongyi Zang, Debasish Ray Mohapatra, Randal Leistikow, Perry R. Cook · Relevance 5.3

VocalTrax recovers interpretable articulatory controls from a recording by gradient descent through a differentiable Pink Trombone synthesizer. Its vocal tract is a Kelly-Lochbaum waveguide updated twice per audio sample, so every gradient step backpropagates through a long recurrence, which is slow on GPUs. We propose Convolutional VocalTrax, which computes the exact frequency response of the same waveguide and applies it as a causal convolution. We assume that the tract is stationary within 5.8 ms blocks and that its lossy impulse response can be truncated after 93 ms. Gradients then pass through FFTs and a short recursion along the tract instead of through time. On 600 AudioMNIST recordings, the tract forward and backward pass runs 47 to 63 times faster on an A100 GPU, and a classifier trained only on real speech recognizes our reconstructions more often than those of VocalTrax (14.5% vs. 10.0%), although the log-mel distance to the target is 11% higher. Parameters fitted with our model keep this gain when rendered by the original VocalTrax (13.8%).

TLDR: Convolutional VocalTrax replaces slow backpropagation through time in differentiable articulatory synthesis by computing the exact frequency response of VocalTrax’s Kelly–Lochbaum vocal tract and applying it as a frozen-block causal convolution with FFT-based gradients, achieving 47–63× faster tract forward/backward passes and more recognizable reconstructions.

JEV-VAD: Efficient Semantic Endpointing via Task-First Conditioning and Decoder-Prefix Pruning

We propose JEV-VAD, an ASR-free framework that combines task-first conditioning, a scalar endpoint readout, and decoder-prefix pruning. The question and completion criteria are placed before audio tokens, allowing causal decoder attention to construct audio states conditioned on the endpointing objective. TLDR: JEV-VAD is an ASR-free semantic endpointing framework that places the endpointing task before audio tokens, uses a scalar stop-probability readout instead of full generation, and prunes the decoder to a shallow refitted prefix to achieve efficient, tunable turn-completion detection with cross-lingual transfer on Qwen3-ASR-0.6B.
